Foundations · Beginner overview

What is an oscilloscope?

An oscilloscope (often abbreviated CRO for Cathode Ray Oscilloscope) is a laboratory instrument that draws a live graph of an electrical signal. The vertical axis of the screen represents voltage; the horizontal axis represents time. The bright line on the screen — the trace — is therefore a plot of voltage versus time, called a waveform.

In this lab the CRO is used with a function generator, which produces known triangular, square, and sinusoidal waves. Measuring those waves on the CRO builds the same skill used whenever a doctor or technician inspects the electrical activity of the heart (ECG), brain (EEG), or muscle (EMG): reading amplitude and timing from a voltage–time display.

How the screen grid works

The CRT screen is marked with a calibrated grid (the graticule). Each square is one division. Two front-panel controls set the scale of those divisions:

Volts / Div (vertical)

Sets how many volts (or millivolts) each vertical division represents. Peak-to-peak height of the wave × volts/div → voltage.

Time / Div (horizontal)

Sets how much time each horizontal division represents (e.g. ms/div or µs/div). Width of one cycle × time/div → period $T$.

PP T (one period) t → V ↑

Graticule reading: vertical divisions × volts/div = voltage; horizontal divisions × time/div = time.

Main front-panel ideas

Intensity & Focus

Intensity sets trace brightness; Focus sharpens the line on the CRT so divisions can be read accurately.

Trigger / sweep match

The time-base sweep is adjusted until the wave appears standing still — matching the sweep rate to the signal frequency.

Deeper background (not from the lab sheet)

In a classic CRO, an electron gun fires a focused beam at a phosphor screen. Vertical deflection plates move the beam with the input voltage; a sawtooth time-base drives the horizontal plates so the spot sweeps left → right at a known rate. Invented by Karl Ferdinand Braun (1897), CROs were standard until digital storage oscilloscopes largely replaced them.

Lab sheet · Clinical context

Medical perspectives

Oscilloscope-style voltage–time displays underpin monitoring of body electrical signals:

  • Monitoring of electrical signals in the body.
  • Use in ECG (heart), EEG (brain), and EMG (muscles).
  • Use in diagnosing neurological and cardiac conditions.
External source

Clinical note

Dedicated clinical ECG/EEG machines amplify and filter microvolt-to-millivolt biosignals and print calibrated strips. General-purpose oscilloscopes appear mainly in medical-device development and teaching, where the same voltage–versus–time reading skill applies.

Lab sheet · Procedure

Method

  1. Turn on the oscilloscope by pushing in the power. It will take a few seconds for a trace to appear on the screen.
  2. Connect the output of the function generator to one of the inputs of the oscilloscope.
  3. Adjust the intensity. This is done by turning the intensity button.
  4. There is a FOCUS knob. Adjust this knob to focus the line in the CRT monitor.
  5. In the waveform generator select a triangular wave and start changing the volt/division button on the oscilloscope front panel until the triangular wave is visible running on the oscilloscope screen.
  6. Start changing the time/division button on the oscilloscope front panel until matching occurs between the frequency of the sweep circuit of the oscilloscope and the frequency of the wave coming from the waveform generator.
  7. Measure the amplitude of the wave using the scale on the oscilloscope screen and multiply by the value of the volt/division button on the front panel of the oscilloscope to get the actual amplitude of the wave in volts or millivolts.
  8. Measure the duration of the wave using the scale on the oscilloscope screen and multiply it by the value of time/division on the front panel of the oscilloscope to get the actual duration of the wave in seconds, milliseconds or microseconds.
  9. Calculate the frequency in Hz, mHz or MHz of the wave from the relation: frequency = 1 / duration.
  10. Repeat the previous steps for square and sinusoidal waves.
  11. Trace the three waveforms.

Lab sheet · Calculations

Observation

1. Amplitude (A)

Peak-to-peak voltage from the vertical scale:

$$V_{PP} = Y\text{ deflection} \times \frac{\text{volts}}{\text{div}}$$

Amplitude (half of peak-to-peak):

$$A = \frac{V_{PP}}{2}\ \text{volt}$$
2. Time period (T)

Duration of one complete cycle from the horizontal scale:

$$T = X\text{ deflection} \times \frac{\text{Time}}{\text{div}}$$
3. Frequency (f)
$$f = \frac{1}{T}\ \text{Hz}$$

Units follow the time-base setting: Hz, kHz, or MHz as appropriate. (Lab sheet also notes mHz where applicable.)

External source

Worked reading example

If one cycle spans 4.0 horizontal divisions at 1 ms/div, then $T = 4.0 \times 1\,\text{ms} = 4.0\,\text{ms} = 0.004\,\text{s}$, so $f = 1/T = 250\,\text{Hz}$. If the same wave spans 3.0 vertical divisions peak-to-peak at 2 V/div, then $V_{PP} = 6.0\,\text{V}$ and $A = 3.0\,\text{V}$.
